### The American Healthcare Paradox

<div >
<img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/41+JVrkKgGL._AC_UF1000,1000_QL80_.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
    This and <span class="fst-italic">Our Malady</span> were books that I procured from a shelf in the office. This one traces out a very pragmatic analysis of why U.S. healthcare outcomes lagging other developed countries despite spending so much more on our medical systems, pharmaceutical research, etc. The long and short of it is that though we spend way more on medicine, we aren't emphasizing non-medical determinants of health nearly as much. In fact, if you factor in money spent on these non-medical determinants (e.g., housing, employment, food security), the U.S. does in fact lag behind other developed countries in holistic-health spending. That is, we spend middle-of-the pack amounts of money on health and therefore have middle-of-the-pack results. One example I distinctly remember from the book is the case of a homeless person suffering an addiction to substances. Because they're likely to have inadequate access to rehab, housing, and employment, they'll continue to worsen until they're suffering an imminent medical emergency and wind up in the emergency room. The individual will undergo an expensive treatment to save their lives because ERs are legally barred from turning people away, and will be discharged from the hospital until they worsen to the point of returning. Each of these visits costs hospitals money and clogs up access to treatment for other people. Though the pressing issue of their addiction's effects are addressed in each hospital visit, the underlying issues go unaddressed and the homeless man's life aren't meaningfully improved each time he goes to the hopsital. This makes sense, hospitals aren't rehab centers, nor do they find someone employment or affordable housing. However, there's extremely limited access to such services in the U.S., so non-medical problems are given band-aid medical solutions while the underlying problems worsen. I won't spend more time simply regurgitating the arguments of the book, but I do want to say that this book was extremely well-balanced in its presentation of its arguments. As a short digression, I recently started watching a Youtube channel called <a href='https://www.youtube.com/@moreperfectunion'>More Perfect Union</a>, which is centered around educating viewers on the issues pressing the working class. I really enjoy their videos on the basis of their educational value, but I dislike the filmographic tactics used to push their agenda. For instance, they'll play scary music in a video of how billionaires are buying up housing, making property ownership inaccessible to the average Joe. It's a scary prospect, surely, but I staunchly believe the facts stand on their own and letting the statistics speak for themselves is more than ample enough to make a convincing argument. That exact issue isn't present in this book. The authors build a strong case for a holistic healthcare in the U.S., and they regularly go out of their way to <a href='https://ratiochristi.org/the-anatomy-of-persuasion-the-steel-man/'>steel-man</a> opposing arguments without resorting to sensationalist vernacular.  I learned a lot from this book and I appreciated the academic approach it took to analyzing the context, problems, and potential solutions facing the American healthcare system. This book isn't like Sula where it aims to make profound philisophical arguments or have the most beautiful prose, but that's ok because it's not the goal of the authors to amaze you with their perceptiveness. This book was clearly elucidated from start to finish and I appreciate that I was encouraged by the interconnectedness of hte arguments to regularly go back to earlier in the book to make sure I followed closely with the arguments being made. A fantastic read and despite it being from over a decade ago, it's issues remain salient and accessible for readers today.
    <br> <br>
    tldr; American healthcare system isn't holistic and I'm glad I know about it beyond news social media posts. 8/10
</p>

### Our Malady: Lessons in Liberty From a Hospital Diary

<div >
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/9/9a/Our_Malady%2C_Lessons_in_Liberty_from_a_Hospital_Diary.jpeg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
    Holy CANNNOOOOOOOOOOOLLLLIIIIIIII. This book knocked my socks off. It's one of, if not the, shortest reads from my summer so far but like <a href='https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toni_Morrison'>the GOAT</a>, Tim Snyder doesn't waste a single letter in his prose.  He opens with a reflection on the rage and empathy he felt while staying in the hospital for liver deterioration and it brilliantly set the stage for the philisophical and anecdotal case he makes for universal healthcare for the majority of the book. My love for this book is vibrantly colored by the more pragmatic case made for holistic healthcare in <span class='fst-italic'>The American Healthcare Paradox</span>. It's one thing to hear a pragmatic breakdown of statistics pointing to what works and what doesn't, be it historically or in different countries. It's another entirely to trace someone's own journey through a profit-centered healthcare system, compare it to his experience in a public-centered healthcare system, reflect on why those differences exist, and recreate the statistics-centered arguments philisophically. One argument I found especially interesting was Snyder's insistence on healthcare being a human right, which the authors of <span class='fst-italic'>The American Healthcare Paradox</span> didn't venture to make. In fact, they made the case that implementing such a change would be nigh-impossible because of American emphasis on individualism and belief that health/socioeconomic struggles are directly tied to the character of the people suffering from them. However, Snyder makes the interesting case that becuase America is, on paper, deeply concerned with securing freedom to all of its citizens, it's necessary for the government to secure healthcare for its population. That is, if you're suffering from chronic illness and don't have adequate care to address it, you're unable to practice your other freedoms in the same capacity as a healthy citizen. However, this is wholly unfair becuase you're suffering from an ailment that your compatriot isn't. You're not as easily disposed to vote, assemble with others to protest, or pursue your happiness. Therefore, because the government aims to secure life, liberty, the pursuit of happiness, and all the rights cascading therefrom, for its citizens, it must secure healthcare for citizens like you. He also makes the case that having unequal access to care encourages a loss of empathy for those who don't share the connections and friends in high places that you do. Snyder mentions in his book that his friends asked him why he didn't leverage his connections to secure better care than the others in the hospitals he was being treated at. Snyder wholly rejects this notion, citing the evil and ambivalence born from being happy or prideful that you're treated as a 1st class citizen at the expense of your fellow human being. The well-connected, in principle, don't deserve to be healthier than the rest of the population by virtue of their socioeconomic status. Isn't that a brilliant argument? I'm certain I didn't do it justice, but that what the original text is for. I implore you to read this book. Forget everything politicians tell you about how you need X or Y because it's what's best for your country. Put that all aside and listen to this Holocaust historian break down the liberty we're deprived of each moment we allow unequal access to healthcare. It's a terrible malady and if <span class='fst-italic'>The American Healthcare Paradox</span> makes you aware of it pragmatically, <span class='fst-italic'>Our Malady</span> makes you furious for your fellow man. Practically speaking, I think that <span class='fst-italic'>The American Healthcare Paradox</span> will better equip you for navigating discussions of American healthcare by virtue of its pragmatic, statistics-oriented approach to unpacking the issue at hand. However, if you want to reflect on your own humanity and the humanity of those you walk uncomfortably past on the street, read <span class='fst-italic'>Our Malady</span>. Truthfully, I massively understated what this book can do for you, but it's so difficult to explain why a masterpiece is a masterpiece when I don't have a fraction of Snyder's syntactic brilliance. If you want this book to really impact you, please educate yourself on the American healthcare system. <span class='fst-italic'>The American Healthcare Paradox</span> is a great place to start and after it blows your mind, this one will give it the <a href='https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar_Bomba'>Tsar bomba</a> treatment.
    <br> <br>
    tldr; Give me liberty or give me death. 10/10
</p>
