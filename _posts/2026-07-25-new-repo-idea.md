My sophomore spring of uni, I took <a href='https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/16-346-astrodynamics-fall-2008/'>16.346 - Astrodynamics</a> with professor Kerri Cahoy. Kerri was a fun professor and 16.346 was far and away my favorite class during a semester that otherwise made me feel like I wasn't cut out to be an engineer. <a href='https://i.ytimg.com/vi/hUAwg7qp2DM/sddefault.jpg'>Ahem.</a> Instead of a final exam, we simulated and presented our choice of term project at the end of the semester. My buddy Avaneesh and I elected to do the default project option because we had no idea what we were doing and wanted heavier guidance. That said, I did most of the actual sim stuff, and that involved learning Orekit. Orekit is a Java library by design for astrodynamics, but it has a python wrapper, which I used to generate the simulations/numbers for our final report. However, I had zero idea what I was doing with the extremely granular library and relied heavily on Claude to write the code up for me [^1].

That said, I realized far too late into the project that there weren't any beginner-friendly orekit tutorials out there. Seriously. Look up <a href='https://www.youtube.com/results?search_query=orekit+tutorial'>Orekit Tutorial</a> on youtube. You won't find jack. It also doesn't help that most people who use Orekit appear to already be fuggin professionals with astrodynamics *and* programming, so whenever I had the technical knowledge to understand some portion of what they were saying, the remainder would be wholly lost on me. This unfruitful search is what led me to basically go all-in on using AI tools to suppleemnt my lackluster understanding of the Orekit library. In hindsight, there's a lot I could've done differently. For instance, I could've opted to use Ansys STK or GMAT to create simulations in, since I had a license through MIT for the former and the latter is straight up free. That's my mistake :sweat: But I digress.

I've been spending the summer trying to hone some skills related to Astrodynamics, and part of that has been going back to learn Orekit "properly." I'd like being competent enough with these tools to restrict my use of AI to boilerplate or assisting in debugging obscure errors. The first thing I tried learning to do was a simple Hohmann transfer, which I thought would be easy since I could just copy and paste a nifty little snippet I found online purporting to do <a href='https://gist.github.com/jorgepiloto/72961771c9a001d3b17b377b841cade1'>just that</a>. However, I learned that this miracle snippet, which I intended to copy by hand and then have AI break down individual snippets to explain to me, was actually produced in a thread on the <a href='https://forum.orekit.org/t/modeling-hohmann-and-bielliptic-maneuvers/1071'>Orekit forums</a> by someone asking why it wasn't working. Incidentally, the gentleman was able to resolve his issue without further posting his fixed code. This would've been fine in 99% of cases since I could simply follow the discussion on the thread to fix the code I copied. *If only.* I banged my head against the wall for a couple hours before relenting and asking Gemini Pro to give it a crack at debugging. It failed.

Eventually, I created a new context window for Gemini and prompt "engineered" it into producing a working script, which I verified by plotting out its results. It made sense geometrically since apsidal burns raise/lower the apsis on the opposite end of the orbit, which is what I saw the plot claim.

![Plot of Hohmann Transfer](/assets/images/gemini_homann.png)

For you know-it-alls who think this is a bielliptic transfer, you're **wrong**. A bielliptic transfer uses two burns and an intermediate orbit to change the altitude of a spacecraft. In this case, the spacecraft starts in an ellptical orbit before raising its perigee with the first burn and circularizing with the second burn.

Digression aside, I realized that I personally would've benefitted greatly from having working scripts like this to reference when I was first doing my Astrodynamics project. Therefore, I've been inspired to create a repo of the tutorials I wish I had before attempting to tackle a programming issue with Orekit, though I anticipate extending this tutorial to things like GMAT scripts since those are also... inadequately... explained online. Hopefully, people with backgrounds similar to mine (i.e., is comfortable programming and has some physical intuition built from other coursework) will find them useful. I pray that it'll be like a lifeline to people who want to learn these really cool tools, but find the current online discussions/resources not particularly useful, locked behind prerequisite knowledge they don't even know is needed.

<a href='https://github.com/GeostationaryBeaver/astro-tutorials'>Here's the link to the repo.</a> The first thing I'll be adding to it is the script Gemini gave me to produce a working Hohmann transfer. However, it'll be modified into a Jupyter notebook so that I can more effectively annotate over it, and because there's some debugging I had to do to make it work in the first place. Fingers crossed this will grow into something people will find useful.
