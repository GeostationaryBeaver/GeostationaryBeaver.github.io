### Lessons in Chemistry

<div >
<img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/71dQACXhz6L._AC_UF1000,1000_QL80_.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
        I found this and the next two books in someone's "FREE" box out on the sidewalk and I thought it was a textbook at first. However, attempting to google it led me to discover this novel's popularity, which as spun it into an HBO mini-series. The series and book appeared to be well-rated online, so I got my hopes up about digging into this book. I understand that writing characters who are experts in subjects that one isn't an expert in themselves can be challenging, but come ON Bonnie Garmus. This is the first book I've ever read where I had to stop multiple times to roll my eyes because I couldn't deal with the corniness. Beleive it or not, intelligence isn't demonstrated by electing obfuscating nomenclature. In fact, intelligent people understand their audiences and know when to use technical terms and when to speak in simplifying analogies. My eyes rolled into the back of my head when Ms. Zott defaulted to calling table salt sodium chloride and vinegar acetic acid. If Zott's vernacular was used in tandem with her technical knowledge to explain the chemistry of the recipes she cooked or drew comparisons between her cooking and labwork, it would've done much more to sell her intelligence without making her sound insufferable. It's such a small thing to nitpick, but it was extremely distracting and I maintain it took away from my enjoyment of the novel. The rest of the book was solid, but it fell into lots of uninteresting tropes and Bonnie Garmus's beliefs were extraordinarily palpable throughout the text. It's glaringly obvious when she asserts the incompatability between science and religion, and she makes no effort to draw serious, balanced discussions on the existence or nature of God. I've seen online influencers produce deeper philisophical takes than "You don't need God to explain anything when you have science :nerd_face:" It only serves to demonstrate Zott's and Garmus's immaturities. I know I'm damn-near mud-raking this book, but those are the things that are unfortunately going to stick with me the longest about it. It's a bit disheartening that <span class='fst-italic'>Lessons in Chemistry</span>'s popularity at least partially lends itself to its audience sharing similar attitudes. I'm starting to get into "erm I'm actually smarter than you" territory here, but I make this criticism because I staunchly believe that these discussions require steel-manning either side's arguments as much as possible. No one is enriched by a one-sided verbal beatdown where the victim is a strawman of a strawman. I realize this review is unnecessarily verbose, but I'd like to also point out that I appreciate Zott being challenged by Evans, who treats her as an intellectual equal. I don't like that his death marks the end of Zott being challenged for any longer than a chapter and marks the end for any meaningful transformation on her part. She begins and ends the story with the same attitudes, never reconsidering her position or being put in a position to challenge her views on marriage, romance, science, religion, etc. In fact, she only acts to change those around her. In the cases of characters like Superman, a trope like this works because he's regularly put into a position where his optimism and ideals are challenged (e.g., <a href='https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/What%27s_So_Funny_About_Truth,_Justice_%26_the_American_Way%3F'>What's so Funny About Truth, Justice, and the American Way?</a>) and his victories are emblematic of why it's others who ought to follow his example. Garmus kinda just assumes you agree with her on every point from the get go and treats every philisophical exchange between characters as a victory lap for her pre-defined beliefs. A good example of this is a conversation between Mad and Wakely, wherein his arc of losing his faith ends with a single "I don't believe in God" whispered into her ear. bruh. Am I seriously supposed to believe that attending a few symposiums, chemistry lectures, and conversations with a literal child were enough to break this dude's faith? It's not necessarily impossible, but I had a hard time reading through it because there was zero effort on Garmus's part to understand why educated religious people exist, or what philisophical arguments there are for the compatability between science and religion. There's also the <a href='https://preview.redd.it/what-does-the-address-me-meme-mean-v0-d2gjo999yddg1.png?auto=webp&s=e8fa7180beac9d80c1021433c7d88811f27ffcbc'>elepahnt in the room</a> I haven't addressed: the novel's feminist themes. It was written in the 2020s about a woman living in the 50s/60s. I initially had a hard time buying how comically sexist everyone was, but some googling revealed that it lowflowkirkenuinely was that bad, so I found it largely convincing and attenable thereafter. Women are smart :smile: I think this novel could've demonstrated that in more creative ways outside of telling me someone reads medical journals recreationally. The exposition dump at the end was also pretty meh. 
        <br><br>
        tldr; Show don't tell and philisophical discussions can in fact be deeper than the conversations held between high schoolers. 6/10
</p>
</div>

### Born to Run

<div >
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/0/0f/Born_to_Run_%28McDougall_book%29.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
    I started running competitively my junior year of high school... at the very end of the cross country season, but I still qualified for CIF as a backup xD. I only raced once that season and it resulted in the nastiest (and come to think of it only?) shin splints I've ever had. I remember what fixed it for me was changing how I ran. Becoming a midfoot striker basically cured the shin splints and I got faster without getting injured thereafter. In fact, I broke the <a href=''>5-minute barrer</a> 2 months after I graduated high school. Suffice to say, I love running, and this book just tickled my brain pink. The Tarahumara are the sickest, most awesome native tribe I've had the pleasure of learning about and learning about them alongside the author was a blast. The book reads like an ivnestigative documentary, wherein we follow our journalist to discover who the Tarahumara are, why Copper Canyons appear to be inhabited by a ghost, and how it all unfolded from the question of "Why does it hurt to run?" I enjoyed the long breakaways that McDougall makes to explain the bits of lore referenced for just long enough for me to ask "and what's that all about?" The Tarahumara are an amazing group of people that have a lot to teach the oustide world, not just runners. Whether it's their quiet compassion or unending hospitality. They love running and I felt a connection with them because I love it too. I definitely caught on when McDougall would take creative liberties with his recounts of events he never would've seen, but I never felt he took it far enough for me to become distracted from the story at hand. That said, I did tire a bit of stories of extremely prodigious runners who suddenly realized how talented they were because I don't find super-talents very inspiring. Is Annie Trason a superhero for holding the <a href='https://www.leadvilleraceseries.com/run/leadvilletrail100run/'>Leadville 100</a> record for nearly 30 years, on top of winning more ultras than I could ever fantasize about competing in? She absolutely is. However, she was presented like a superwoman type of character, and though she is, I feel that more credit is due to her work ethic to have become such a powerhouse. In general, I don't care to learn much about naturally talented people doing talentful things. Even if it seems that way on the surface, the Tarahumara are absolutely not an example of this. They live, breathe, and eat running. I unironically started drinking water with chia seeds mixed into it because of what I read in this book. I need to get ahold of Pinole too. That sounds fire. I haven't talked much on how the book itself unraveled, but honestly I don't feel like I have to (and I'm realizing that I have to write about the other 3 books too). This book was extremely enjoyable, accessible, and educational. I don't think I would've learned for a long time why the fastest land-animals on Earth run on 4 legs instead of 2 (it helps them supply large quantities of oxygen quickly to their bodies for their sprints, among other things). I don't have any complaints, but I feel strange about giving it a 10/10 because I don't know if it affected me deeply enough to give it a rating that high,
    <br><br>
    tldr; I love running/learning and so does the author of this book. 8.5/10
</p>
</div>

### Eleanor Oliphant is Completely Fine

<div >
<img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/413XS+HZuML._SL500_.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
    The blurb on this one read like that of a Hallmark movie, so it was only my trust of the Penguin Publishing label that kept me from further preemptive judgement of this book. The first fifth of the book was kind of unbearable for me because I profoundly disliked Eleanor. Check out the aside for some more context on what I'll be saying next (markdown decides where it goes and I'm too lazy to move it manually). That said, I very quickly latched onto any signs of likability in her character, and I rushed to sympathize with her when I realized how much she'd gone through in her childhood. This also may have been me projecting, but Eleanor, to me, was pretty clearly coded as neurodivergent, which made me feel far more comfortable with her frankness about criticizing parts of society that were arbitrary to her. This isn't to say that neurotypical people aren't allowed to point out how silly it is that women need a whole <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iym5TVomn-I'>power rangers suit up sequence</a> to prepare for any outing, but Eleanor's way of expressing that tension is much more fun to follow along with when I feel I share the same "source" of those criticisms. Though I don't relate to a fraction of the trauma Eleanor's survived, I still increasingly saw myself in her as the novel progressed. Further, this has to be my favorite execution of a romance story. Ever. That should mean something because I read YA novels like there was no tomorrow in middle and high school. Yeah that's right, I read <span class='fst-italic'>Cinder</span> and <span class='fst-italic'>The Young Elites</span>. Sue me (I can't let the homies know I fw Marissa Meyer... I need to Reread <span class='fst-italic'>Renegades</span> and finish the rest of the series). Ahem. What I appreciate about the book's romantic subplot is that it kinda takes a backseat to the reader uncovering Eleanor's character and past, and it's not epitomized with a sex scene. Gah-LEE that was refreshing. A romance spawned out of a friendship, where the reader watches the friendship grow organically and the romance is encoded with just a sweet kiss on the cheek at the <span class='fst-italic'>verrryyy</span> end of the book? YES. I'm so sick of romance being "let's be friends for a week and then sex each other until we decide to get married" in popular media. That's not what love is, love is friendly bickering and banter and having a shoulder to lean on and Gail Honeyman CRUSHED demonstrating this. Eleanor spends most of the novel fantasizing over a parasocial relationship with some rando, but the reader knows DAMN well she'll end up with Raymond because we watch them become best friends organically. Honeyman forgoes the usual escalation of physical intimacy to match emotional intimacy and instead takes a "and the rest is history" approach to Eleanor and Raymond's relationship, trusting that we'll put two and two together at the novel's end. That's so unbelievably refreshing to me. I wish more romances were portrayed like this. It's ironic that I gush about the novel's romantic arc right after saying it wasn't the main focus, but that's just a testament to the novel's solidity. I feel strange rating how the author tackles trauma and mental issues I've never known and will (hopefully) never know, but from what I've read, my instinct agrees with the consensus that Honeyman portrays mental health quite accurately. It reminds me of 21M.283, the musicals class I took last Spring. In it, we covered <a href='https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7r8OWYElBho'>Next to Normal</a>, which also portrayed mental health struggles in a raw, realistic way. The musical jumps in with both feet :wink: into the "nightmare" scenario of a family confronting its mental health struggles, and I connect the two works together because they don't sanitize anything. The world keeps turning regardless if the characters overcome themselves or not, and it's deeply comforting when they find ways to navigate their unideal situations. I grew to care about Eleanor a great deal after finding her quite distasteful at first. That's generally a hard thing to do as a writer. Honeyman deserves her flowers for this book. It's great.
    <br><br>
    tldr; Romance and mental health are portrayed extremely well. 8/10
</p>

### Sula

<div >
<img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/S/compressed.photo.goodreads.com/books/1632284105i/11347.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
    My girlfiend, Liz, is a big Toni Morrison fan. I mean she is THE Toni Morrison fan. She's modest though, she won't admit she knows she's the biggest Toni Morrison fan, but I know better. That said, she had two copies of the same book gifted to her on her (I think it was 18th?) birthday, and she lent me her extra copy to read. HOLY. MOLY. Toni Morrison's got <a href='https://aas.princeton.edu/people/toni-morrison'>hella hardware</a> and reading Sula demonstrated pretty damn well why. There isn't a single wasted letter in any of this book. What I love love LOVE about Sula, and what I'd assume is Toni Morrison's general writing style, is its incessant commitment to showing instead of telling. I'm reminded of studio Ghibli films when I read Sula because they share a common disinterest in the standard format of rising-action, climax, falling-action, opting instead for a slice of life approach. That is, reading Sula made me feel like I was sitting just across from Helene Wright when she flashed her dazzling smile to the frustration of the other black passengers. There wasn't a single point in this book where I felt like it was boring to look into these characters' lives, because Toni Morrison details them so convincingly. This was the only book I've read so far where I found myself consistently flipping to earlier chapters because I recalled a small detail in the dialogue between two characters which informed the actions one of those same characters later on. This book was a challenging read because of the syntax, but I didn't feel that it came from a place of self-indulgence. Morrison's extremely particular about her word selection and anything you shift to the left or right would carry ramifications for the rest of the book. Little details like Eva's resolved being steeled by the blood-stained water she sips while mistaking it for soda make Plum's death even more heartbreaking. Eliminating just the sentence where she drops the cup to the floor eliminates the moment where Eva is finally driven over the edge. She walked into Plum's room to say goodbye, unbeknownst to him, and her conflict is resolved just long enough for her to light Plum on fire. We don't get a play-by-play of Eva's thoughts, only seeing her actions and a snippet of her memories so we can draw our own conclusions. I'm sure someone more well-versed in Morrison's literature could correct me on the interpretation I have of Plum's death, but they'll agree regardless to the scene's depth. That itself encapsulates something else I love about her literature, it's deep enough to facilitate discussion on even the briefest snippets. All that said, and I want to be ginger about this next thought, I think of myself outside the "target audience" for Morrison's work, and I felt that in the cultural detachment I felt from the ensemble cast. Though Morrison knocks it out of the park in portraying each character organically and earnestly, I struggled to connect with the underlying culture of the Bottom. It's moreso a fault of my comprehension and empathy, but it's my own thing preventing a perfect rating.
    <br><br>
    tldr; Toni Morrison's the goat, but I'm still not fully literate. 9/10 
</p>

### Is God a Mathematician?

<div >
<img src="https://m.media-amazon.com/images/I/81eOwFY79aL._UF1000,1000_QL80_.jpg" class="img-fluid rounded w-50" alt="Responsive image" style="float: left; margin-right: 15px; margin-bottom: 10px;">
<p>
    I'm tired of writing long reviews and I don't think I can be bothered. My girlfriend lent me this book the first time I visited her for an extended period of time and it sat in my backpack for embarrasingly long before I decided I would spend my commute to MITRE reading it. I enjoyed this book, even if it felt hand-wavy about the philosophy it wanted to tackle and the math concepts at play. I also don't like the "erm maybe BOTH sides are right" attitude the author takes at the end of the book. It's the easiest position to claim and though he makes a fair case for "math is invented, but we make discoveries about what our ruleset teaches about the universe," it reads more like "I think math is invented, but I don't want to alienate anyone who's gotten this far into my book." Even so, I loved the premise of starting from the Greek philosophers and tracing the debate of math's discovery/invention from then until the modern day. I learned a lot about historical figures associated with mathematics and I felt cathartic when I could see which direction the author was going to take before he ended up there, thanks to my own background knowledge. Admittedly, it's this same background knowledge which made me certain of the author's final position being predestined to be that math was strictly invented. I figured he'd take this stance because of Godel's Incompleteness Theorem, which is functionally a death-blow to the idea that math is self-sufficient. Maybe it's my hubris that won't let go of the author's final position, but come ON, Mario. Regardless, I really enjoyed this book and my summer was definitely enriched because of it.
    <br><br>
    tldr; Bro I didn't write that much come on. 8/10
</p>
