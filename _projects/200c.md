Skills: Circuit wiring, CAD, power tools, public speaking, long-distance collaboration, client relationship management

After taking [Terrascope](/projects/terrascope.html) in the Fall of my freshman year, I was given the option to take a related class called [2.00C, Design for Complex Environmental Issues](https://terrascope.mit.edu/portfolio_page/1-016/). We were to continue on an aspect of our previous semester's project, and my cohort did that with the raising awareness angle. Additionally, we would complete this project in collaboration with students at [Diné College](https://www.dinecollege.edu/) and the [University of Puerto Rico at Ponce](https://www.uprp.edu/en/). Our client was the [Nevada Museum of Art](https://www.nevadaart.org/), and our point of contact was its Senior VP of Education and Research, Colin Robertson.

The overarching goal of the semester, broken up into several sprints, was to create an art museum exhibit which facilitated deeper thought about the art shown. Importantly, we had to do so while minimizing how "gadgety" our projects were. That is, our exhibit should draw people towards the art and not the tool focusing their attention. For example, having an exhibit which shows off art by letting you change what color spotlight is pointed at an art piece is quickly going to become distracting from the art as users play more with the lights than think about the choice of color in the piece.

The semester was split into 9 sprints. We spent the first $\frac23$ of the course working on sprints partitioned out for us, before planning and completing our own sprints. At the end of the semester, we presented our final project in the Bazaar of Ideas. In the background, we also spent a few class sessions building wooden benches to get accustomed to the skills needed further down the line.

### Sprint Breakdown

1. Meeting with Colin Robertson for the first time, we created a [client profile](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint1_profile.pdf) of who he was, what his interests were for this project, and his specifications for the final deliverable. 
2. Created toy models as [first passes](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint2_models.pdf) for what our final design could be. Here, we first fleshed out the idea of sectioning off art pieces according to shared themes using a console that users interact with. The MIT toy model is closer to the design we ultimately used, despite scrapping the multi-sense experience we originally went for.
3. Before continuing to pursue any single idea, we came up with multiple drafts of problem statements individually before [unifying them](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint3_pstatements.pdf) into a single problem statement and approach. Additionally, we iterated on a problem tree analysis to reevaluate what kind of project we wanted to build. 
4. This was the sprint where we settled on and started iterating over the [first draft](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint4_mechanisms.pdf) of what would become our final design. We forewent the needlessly novel full-sensory experience with a variable display. In short, the art pieces would sit in a box, obscured from view, until the user switched a lever related to a certain theme. The box would then display an art piece related to that theme, encouraging the user to play around with the levers and figure out why certain art pieces were selected to correspond with certain lever configurations. The 3 different core functionalities were split among the 3 campuses to demonstrate at the next standup.
5. This was our last sprint before demonstrating our first prototypes in-person during the spring break Terrascope road trip from Las Vegas to Salt Lake City. One of the stops we made was in Reno to the Nevada Musem of Art. Prior to presenting, we made [an outline](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint5_pp.pdf) of what we wanted to highlight about our design.
    - I was particularly frustrated by the feedback we received at the time, feeling that calling our design too "gadgety" was ridiculous. However, it served as a good starting point to strip the design back further and dissecting what about it was truly necessary. Extracting the *philosophy* of our initial design allowed us to refactor it into the final design.
6. After going back to the drawing board, we came up with a [new schematic](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint6_selections.pdf) for our design which eliminated all moving parts. Instead of a rotating display of individual art pieces, we'd arrange a potpourri of pieces which would become differently backlit depending on the theme selected by a user. That is, instead of one art piece appearing for each theme while the others were obscured, all the art pieces would be immediately visible and a small emphasis would be placed on certain artworks via a backlight. With this new configuration, users could contemplate the connections between art pieces along with considering why individual pieces were selected to fit a theme.
7. This sprint was spent CADing the frames used for the art, creating circuit diagrams to understand the wiring, ordering parts available to each campus, and justifying our choices of art. The sketches which turned into our CAD models and final design can be [found here.](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint7_retro.pdf) One of the key challenges in this sprint was ensuring all of our teammates could create the same final product by creating a bill of materials which only sourced from stores local to each of the 3 campuses. 
8. This spring was spent [furthering the work](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint8_restrospec.pdf) of the previous sprint, finalizing construction choices and beginning the final stretch of assembly.  
9. This was the last stretch of grunge work now that all of our parts were [made and distributed](/assets/portfolio/200c/sprint9_physical.pdf). All that was left was assembling the final design and rehearsing our pitch to Colin Robertson, emphasizing the changes we made in response to his feedback.
